ACCESSICOOK
Oven Skills
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
The oven gives you even heat and free hands once the food is in, but the racks, the door, and the pans are serious burn hazards. This chapter covers rack position, preheating, putting food in and taking it out, and judging doneness without staring through the window.

SET THE RACKS FIRST
Place oven racks before you preheat. A middle rack is the default in this book unless a recipe says otherwise. For many sheet-pan meals and cookies, the center is best. For a pie that needs a browner bottom, a slightly lower rack can help. For a casserole that browns on top, a slightly higher rack can help.

Feel the rack positions when the oven is cold. Count the slots from the bottom so you can repeat the same setting. If you need to move a rack, do it before the oven is hot.

PREHEAT
Preheat unless the recipe, or a package such as some turkey bacon, says to start in a cold oven. Many ovens beep or chime when they reach temperature; learn that sound, and still give the oven a couple of extra minutes if your oven runs cool. A tactile mark or bump dot on 350, 375, 400, and 425 degrees F will cover most of this book.

Decide where the hot pan will land before you open the door. Clear that space and set out long, dry oven mitts.

LOADING AND UNLOADING
Stand to the side as you open the door so the first roll of steam does not hit your face. Pull the rack partly out with mitts, set or remove the pan, and close the door. Do not reach deep into a hot oven with bare forearms.

If you cannot tell whether the pan is centered, ask a helper to confirm, or feel the rack edges with a mitted hand and locate the pan relative to the rack, not by reaching over the food.

Use a talking timer every time something is in the oven. Set it when the food goes in, not after you think of it.

DONENESS IN THE OVEN
A thermometer is the right tool for meat, poultry, casseroles, and leftovers. For baked goods, use time, aroma, and touch: a cake often springs back lightly in the center, a toothpick or skewer comes out without wet batter (a few moist crumbs can be fine), and the kitchen smells baked, not raw and floury or sharp and burnt. A sighted helper can add color cues, such as golden brown, but color alone is not enough for meat.

If you open the door to check, do it quickly so the oven does not cool too much, and start with a thermometer or a touch test rather than a long look.

WHEN YOU ARE FINISHED
Turn the oven off. Leave hot pans on the reserved landing zone. Tell anyone else in the home that a hot pan is there. Let the oven cool before you wipe it.
