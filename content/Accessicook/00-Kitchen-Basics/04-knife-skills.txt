Knife Skills, the Claw Grip, and Cut-Resistant Gloves
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
Most recipes in this book ask you to cut something: an onion, a chicken breast, a loaf of bread. A calm, repeatable method matters more than speed. This chapter covers a stable board, the claw grip, a cut-resistant glove, and a few safer alternatives when a large knife is not the right tool.

SET UP THE BOARD
Put a damp dish towel or a non-slip mat under a sturdy cutting board so it cannot slide. Give yourself enough counter space that your elbows are not pinned to your sides. Use a board reserved for raw meat when you are cutting meat or poultry, or wash the board and your hands thoroughly before they touch ready-to-eat food.

THE CLAW GRIP
The hand that holds the food is the claw hand. Tuck your fingertips under and curl your fingers so the fingertips are behind the knuckles, with the thumb tucked behind as well. The side of the knife blade rests against those knuckles, which act as a fence. The fingertips stay back and out of the way.

The other hand holds the knife with a secure grip on the handle. Confirm the sharp edge is down by feeling the spine, which is the dull top of the blade, before you start. Cut down and slightly forward, then move the claw hand backward a little at a time. Do not rush. Let the knife finish one cut before you set up the next.

A cut-resistant glove on the claw hand adds a layer of protection while you learn, and many experienced cooks keep wearing one for raw poultry and slippery foods. It is not a reason to be careless with the blade. The glove does not belong on the knife hand.

SAFER CUTTING HABITS
Feel the item, then set it on a flat, stable side if it has one. Cut a thin slice off a round food such as an onion or potato to make a flat face, then rest that face on the board so the food cannot roll. For garlic, you can loosen the paper skin by pressing the clove under the flat of the knife, with your hand on the blade spine, never on the sharp edge.

Move trimmings out of the work area so you do not cut through a pile of scraps. If the knife feels like it is slipping, stop, dry the handle, and dry the food.

WHEN NOT TO USE A CHEF'S KNIFE
A vegetable peeler is the better tool for peeling carrots and potatoes. Work away from your body and rotate the food. Kitchen shears are useful for snipping herbs, cutting raw bacon, and opening packages. A pizza cutter can slice a sandwich or a quesadilla. If chopping an onion is the step that stalls you, a hand chopper or already-diced onion from the store is a fair substitution.

KEEPING KNIVES SAFE WHEN YOU ARE NOT CUTTING
Set a knife down with the blade facing away from the edge of the counter. Do not drop it into a sink full of soapy water, where it cannot be seen or felt safely. Wash it individually, dry it, and put it back in its block, sheath, or magnetic strip.

This chapter draws on standard culinary teaching, including the claw-grip guidance used in professional training materials such as the Institute of Child Nutrition's knife-skills resources, and on common adaptive-kitchen practice.
