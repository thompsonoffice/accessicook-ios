============================================================
ACCESSICOOK
Adaptive Tools and Where to Find Them
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
The right tools do not cook the meal for you, but they make measuring, timing, and doneness much more reliable. This chapter lists the tools used most often in Accessicook, what they do, and how to think about buying them. You do not need every item on day one.

TOOLS THAT SPEAK OR TACTILE-MARK
A talking or large-display instant-read thermometer is the most important safety tool in this book. Use it for meat, poultry, egg dishes, casseroles, and leftovers. A fry or probe thermometer is worth adding if you will heat oil.

A talking or tactile timer keeps you from burning food when you cannot watch a clock. A phone timer works if the phone stays away from the heat and you can hear it.

A talking kitchen scale measures flour, meat, and cheese without a stack of similar cups. Tactile or large-print measuring cups and spoons still matter for liquids and small amounts. An electronic liquid level indicator hangs on the rim of a cup, bowl, or pot and beeps or vibrates as the liquid rises.

Bump dots, Hi-Mark or similar tactile pens, braille labels, and talking labelers all work on jars and appliance knobs. Use whatever system you will actually maintain.

TOOLS THAT PROTECT YOUR HANDS
A cut-resistant glove goes on the hand that holds the food, not the knife hand. Long oven mitts protect wrists and forearms in the oven. A splatter screen cuts down grease burns on a skillet. A lid that fits your main pot should live within reach in case of a grease flare-up. Cool-touch oven rack guards, if you can find them, reduce forearm burns when you reach into the oven.

TOOLS THAT MAKE PREP EASIER
A sharp chef's knife is safer than a dull one, because it is less likely to slip. A vegetable peeler is safer than peeling with a knife. A pizza cutter or a rocking knife can be easier than a long slicing stroke when you are learning. A pair of tongs, a wooden spoon, and a heat-safe spatula will get you through most of this book. A double spatula, sometimes sold as a "safety food turner," helps flip fish or an egg without chasing it around the pan.

WHERE TO LOOK
Specialty products are sold by blindness organizations and independent living stores, including the National Federation of the Blind Independence Market and many state or private agencies. Ordinary kitchen stores and large retailers also sell talking thermometers, timers, scales, cut-resistant gloves, splatter screens, and long mitts. A tool does not have to be marketed as "adaptive" to be useful.

If cost is a barrier, start with a thermometer, a timer, one tactile set of measuring spoons, a cut-resistant glove, and bump dots. Add the rest as you need them.

KEEPING TOOLS IN WORKING ORDER
Replace the batteries in talking tools before they fail in the middle of a recipe. Dry thermometers and liquid level indicators as the package directs. Keep knives sharp. Wash cut-resistant gloves as the maker allows; some are machine washable, and some are not.
