============================================================
ACCESSICOOK
Setting Up an Accessible Kitchen
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
You do not need a special kitchen to cook from this book. You do need a layout you can trust, a few habits that keep heat and knives predictable, and labels on the things you cannot identify by shape or smell. This chapter is a setup guide. You can change a kitchen over a weekend, then improve it as you cook.

GIVE EVERYTHING A HOME
Keep the cutting board, knives, mixing bowls, measuring tools, salt, oil, timers, and thermometers in the same places every time. A consistent layout is faster and safer than searching with a burner on.

Store heavy pans on a low, sturdy shelf. Keep spices and everyday oils where you can reach them without stretching over a hot stove. If you share the kitchen with a helper, agree on the layout and put it back the same way after cleaning.

CLEAR THE COUNTERS
Before you cook, move mail, devices, and extra dishes off the work surface. Clutter is how pans get bumped and knives get found the hard way. Leave one clear landing zone for hot pans, and do not use that zone for anything else while you cook.

LABEL WHAT YOU CANNOT IDENTIFY
Use bump dots, tactile marking pens, braille labels, or a talking labeler on spice jars, oil bottles, and pantry staples that feel similar. Mark the oven and stove settings you use most often, such as medium heat, 350, 375, 400, and 425 degrees F. If the knobs are smooth, one raised dot at each of those positions is enough.

A talking scale reduces the number of similar-feeling cups you have to keep track of. A liquid level indicator beeps or vibrates as a cup or pot fills.

SET UP WORK ZONES
Create a raw-meat zone and a ready-food zone. Use one cutting board and one set of tools for raw meat and poultry, and a different board for bread, vegetables, cheese, and cooked food. If you have only one board, wash it with soap and hot water, and wash your hands, before it touches anything that will not be cooked again.

Put a damp dish towel or a non-slip mat under the cutting board. Keep a trash bowl on the raw zone for wrappers and trimmings so you are not crossing the kitchen with chicken packaging.

DRESS FOR THE STOVE
Wear short sleeves or roll long sleeves above the elbow. Avoid loose cuffs, dangling jewelry, and long loose hair that can catch a handle or dip into a pan. Tie back anything that hangs. Use long oven mitts, not damp towels, on hot pans.

LIGHTING AND CONTRAST, FOR LOW VISION AND HELPERS
If you have some usable vision, or if you cook with a sighted helper, high-contrast tools help: a dark board for light foods, a light board for dark foods, and a well-lit, uncluttered counter. This book does not rely on that contrast, but it is useful when it is available.

A STARTER DRAWER OF TOOLS
You can cook most of this book with:

- A talking or instant-read thermometer
- A tactile or talking timer
- Measuring spoons and cups with tactile markings, plus a liquid level indicator
- A cut-resistant glove
- Long oven mitts
- A splatter screen
- One stable cutting board and one sharp chef's knife
- A large pot, a heavy skillet or Dutch oven, a rimmed baking sheet, and an 8-inch nonstick skillet

Add a talking scale, a box grater or pre-grated cheese, a colander, tongs, and a wooden spoon as you go. Adaptive tools are covered in more detail in the next chapter.
