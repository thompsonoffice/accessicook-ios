Stovetop Skills, Including Flat-Top Ranges
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
The stove is where most weeknight cooking happens, and it is also where most burns happen. This chapter covers finding a burner, setting a pan down, judging heat without watching a flame, and working on a smooth flat-top range.

FIND THE BURNER BEFORE YOU TURN IT ON
Position the pan on a cold burner first, then turn the heat on. Stand square to the front of the stove. Keep pot handles turned inward, but not over another burner.

To find a burner without placing your hand on a hot surface, use a long wooden spoon as a feeler, or learn a "safety zone" heat check: keep your hand at least midriff height, lower it slowly, and feel for rising heat rather than touching the grate or the glass. Some cooks mark the center of each burner on a wooden spoon with tactile tape, measuring once from the front edge of the stove.

JUDGING HEAT
Medium heat is the default in many of these recipes. On many electric and flat-top stoves, the middle of the knob is a useful tactile mark. The pan tells you more than the knob does:

- A drop of water should sizzle and skitter on a hot, empty skillet, then dry quickly, when you are preheating for searing.
- Butter should melt, then foam, and smell nutty, not acrid or burnt.
- Oil should flow easily and smell hot, not sharp or smoky. If oil smokes, the pan is too hot. Take it off the heat.
- A gentle simmer sounds like small, regular bubbles. A boil is louder and more rattling. A hard, spattering boil usually means you should turn the heat down.

If food smells scorched, remove the pan from the heat first, then decide what to do. Turning the food is easier once the burner is not still blasting.

BOILING WATER AND STEAM
Fill a pot no more than halfway to two-thirds full. You can feel the water line with a clean finger before the heat is on, or use a liquid level indicator on the rim. Add salt after the water is hot if you like, keeping your face back from steam. Add pasta or vegetables carefully so the water does not splash. If a pot sounds like it is boiling over, turn the heat down and, if needed, slide it off the burner.

Turn the heat off before you move a full, heavy pot. If a pot of water is too heavy to lift safely, cook a smaller amount, or scoop some water out with a mug before you drain.

FLAT-TOP RANGES
Use flat-bottomed, stable pans. Keep the glass clear of paper, food wrappers, and plastic. The surface stays hot after the burner is off; treat the whole top with caution until it has cooled. Always turn the heat off before you move a pan. Wipe spills after the surface is cool enough, following the maker's directions, because sugar syrups can damage some glass tops.

GAS RANGES
If you cook with gas, learn how your starter sounds and how the knob feels when the burner is lit. If you smell gas and the burner is not lit, turn it off, do not use a flame or unplug anything, and follow your local gas-safety procedure. Confirm the flame is actually on before you walk away, using heat, sound, and, for a helper, a visual check.

NEVER LEAVE HIGH-RISK COOKING UNATTENDED
Hot oil, bacon, popcorn, and empty pans on high heat need you to stay in the kitchen. Set a timer even when you think you will remember.
