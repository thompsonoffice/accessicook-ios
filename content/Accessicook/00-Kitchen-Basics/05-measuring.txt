Measuring by Touch, Sound, and Weight
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
Accurate measuring makes baking rise and keeps seasoning under control. You can measure well without seeing the line on a cup. This chapter covers dry measures, liquid measures, spoons, scales, and a few tricks for salt, oil, and spices.

DRY INGREDIENTS
Use individual or nesting dry measuring cups with raised, large-print, or braille markings. To fill a dry cup, spoon the ingredient in and level it with a straight edge, such as the back of a knife, feeling for the rim of the cup. Do not pack flour. Brown sugar is the usual exception: pack it until it holds the shape of the cup when you turn it out.

If two cups feel similar, mark them with bump dots or keep only one set, stored in order. A talking scale is often easier for flour, sugar, and oats. When a recipe in this book gives cups, you can use a standard conversion if you prefer weight, such as about 120 grams for 1 cup of unsifted all-purpose flour, but not every ingredient weighs the same per cup, so use a reliable conversion for that ingredient.

LIQUIDS
Use a liquid measuring cup, which has a pouring spout, together with an electronic liquid level indicator. Clip or hang the indicator on the rim and pour slowly until it beeps or vibrates at the level you want. You can also pour over a kitchen sink or a tray to catch spills, and dip a clean finger just over the rim to feel when a cup is nearly full, but the indicator is more precise and more sanitary for stocks and milk.

For a small amount, such as 1 tablespoon of oil or soy sauce, tactile measuring spoons are easier than a cup.

SPOONS
Tactile or well-marked spoons let you feel 1/4, 1/2, and 1 teaspoon, and 1 tablespoon. Fill the spoon and level dry spices with a finger or a straight edge. For liquids, fill to the brim but do not overflow. Wash the spoon between a strong spice such as cayenne and a mild one such as sugar, or keep two spoons in play.

SALT, PEPPER, AND OIL
Many savory recipes in this book give an amount of salt and then tell you to taste a cooled bite at the end. If you are learning, measure the salt. A pinch is roughly 1/8 teaspoon (an eighth of a teaspoon). When you are comfortable, you can salt from a small dish rather than over the pan, which prevents a stuck shaker lid from dumping a load into the food.

Oil can be measured in a spoon or poured in a slow count you practice once with a spoon nearby. For frying, though, measure, because the amount of oil affects safety.

WHEN TO TASTE
Taste only food that is safe to taste: not raw meat, not raw eggs, not raw batter or dough with eggs or flour, and not a boiling spoonful that will burn you. Cool a small spoonful, then taste and adjust salt, acid, or heat.
