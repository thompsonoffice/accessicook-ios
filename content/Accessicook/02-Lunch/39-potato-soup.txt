Potato Soup
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
A creamy soup of potatoes, onion, and milk or broth. You can leave it chunky or mash some of the potatoes in the pot to thicken it, with no extra flour.

SERVINGS
4 to 6

TOTAL TIME
About 40 minutes

DIFFICULTY
Beginner

EQUIPMENT
- Measuring cups and spoons with tactile markings
- A liquid measuring cup and a liquid level indicator when you are measuring liquids
- A tactile or talking timer
- A stable cutting board with a damp towel underneath
- A sharp chef's knife
- A cut-resistant glove for the hand that holds the food
- A large pot with a lid
- A wooden spoon or ladle
- A talking or instant-read food thermometer

INGREDIENTS
- 2 tablespoons butter
- 1 onion, diced
- 2 garlic cloves, minced
- 2 pounds potatoes, peeled and cut into 1 inch cubes
- 4 cups chicken or vegetable broth
- 1 cup milk
- 1 teaspoon salt, plus more to taste
- 1/4 teaspoon (a quarter teaspoon) black pepper
- Optional toppings: shredded cheese, cooked bacon, or chopped green onion

TACTILE TIPS
Potato cubes should be even. A done cube mashes easily. The finished soup should feel thick and creamy, with some soft chunks if you like it that way.

BEFORE YOU START
Read the whole recipe once before you turn on the heat. Set out the equipment and ingredients. Clear a landing space for hot pans. If you will be cutting, put a damp towel under the cutting board. If you are cooking with a helper, decide who will handle knives, the stove, and the thermometer before you start.

STEPS
1. Dice the onion and any other vegetables listed. Dice means cut into small, even cubes.

2. Mince the garlic. Mince means cut into very small pieces.

3. Heat the oil or butter in a large pot over medium heat.

4. Add the onion, and the other aromatics.

5. Cook, stirring, until they feel soft and smell sweet, about 4 to 6 minutes.

6. Add the potatoes, broth, and salt.

7. Bring the pot to a simmer. Simmer means cook just below a boil, with small, gentle bubbles.

8. Simmer 15 to 20 minutes, until the potatoes mash easily.

9. Mash some of them in the pot, or blend part of the soup, then stir in the milk and pepper.

10. Heat gently. Do not boil hard after the milk goes in.

11. Taste a cooled spoonful and add salt if it needs it.

12. Serve hot.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE
Non-visual cues: A potato cube should mash easily. The soup should smell buttery and savory. If you add bacon as a topping, it should already be fully cooked.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The potatoes should look soft and falling apart at the edges if you want a thick soup. The soup should look pale and creamy, not gray and gluey, and not thin and watery.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
Say what you see out loud, especially color and browning. Share any thermometer reading with your partner. Do not skip the temperature checks in the steps. Visual cues help, but they do not replace a thermometer for meat, poultry, egg dishes, or leftovers. The potatoes should look soft and falling apart at the edges if you want a thick soup. The soup should look pale and creamy, not gray and gluey, and not thin and watery.

SAFETY
The pot is hot. If you use an immersion blender, keep it under the surface so it does not splatter. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F, stirring so the bottom does not scorch. This dish has no raw meat. Still keep perishable ingredients cold. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F.

TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Russet potatoes make a softer, thicker soup. Yukon Golds hold their shape a little more. See Loaded toppings like cheese and cooked bacon if you want a baked-potato feel.

LEFTOVERS
Refrigerate perishable leftovers within 2 hours of cooking, or within 1 hour if the room is hotter than 90 degrees F. Use shallow containers so the food cools faster. Eat refrigerated leftovers within 3 to 4 days. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F. Bring leftover soups, sauces, and gravies back to a boil.
