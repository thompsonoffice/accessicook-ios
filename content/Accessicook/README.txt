============================================================
ACCESSICOOK
README
============================================================

Welcome to Accessicook, an accessible cookbook written so a blind or low-vision cook and a sighted in-home care worker, or any other helper, can cook from the same pages. The recipes are plain text, easy to follow by ear or on a braille display, and written as real cooking lessons, not just ingredient lists.

The book is organized into Kitchen Basics plus six cooking categories:

- Kitchen Basics: 13 teaching chapters on using the book, setting up the kitchen, tools, knives, measuring, the stove, the oven, the microwave, the air fryer, food safety, shopping and labeling, cleanup, and a first-week practice plan
- Breakfast
- Lunch
- Supper
- Baked Goods
- Midnight Snacks
- Misc: sides, sauces, appetizers, drinks, and staples that do not belong to a single meal

HOW TO START
Read this README, then the first Kitchen Basics chapter, "How to Use This Book with VoiceOver, NVDA, and a Braille Display." If you are cooking with a helper, also read the section below on cooking together. When you are ready to cook, use INDEX.txt to find a recipe, or open CONTENTS.txt and move through the book in order. Accessicook-all-recipes.txt is one combined file with every chapter and recipe, useful for searching a single document.

All files are plain UTF-8 text. There are no tables and no steps that depend on color alone. Temperatures are written as "165 degrees F" so a screen reader says them clearly.

HOW A RECIPE IS SET UP
Every recipe uses the same headings, in this order: introduction, servings, total time, difficulty, equipment, ingredients, tactile tips, before you start, numbered steps, how to tell when it is done, a short note for a sighted helper, safety, tips and variations, leftovers, and sources.

Doneness is described in two ways. Non-visual cues use sound, smell, texture, time, and a thermometer. Visual cues are written for a sighted helper to say out loud. They do not replace the thermometer on meat, poultry, egg dishes, or leftovers.

TIPS FOR COOKING WITH A HELPER
Accessicook is meant to be used together, not handed off. A good default is that the cook who wants to learn or stay in practice leads, and the helper supports.

Agree on roles before any heat is on. One clear split is:

- The lead cook sets the pace, handles as much measuring, cutting, and stove work as they want to do, and decides when to check doneness.
- The helper reads the next step out loud when asked, confirms visual cues, double-checks a thermometer reading, spots hot pans, and helps with labels or package directions.

Ask for a specific check, such as "What is the thermometer reading, and does the chicken look opaque in the center?" rather than "Is it done?" Both of you should hear the number. If you disagree, measure again in a second place.

Do not silently take over a step. If you need to jump in for safety, say so, and then return the task if your partner wants it back. Praise and corrections land better when they are specific: "The onions smell sweet and look translucent, so I think we are ready for the garlic," not "Here, let me do that."

Keep the phone or braille display back from burners and sinks. A talking timer is safer than handling a device over a hot pan. Decide in advance where hot pans will land, and say "hot pan" when one moves.

If one of you is new to the other's kitchen, walk the space once before you cook: board, knives, thermometer, timer, salt, oil, mitts, and the landing zone.

A BASIC ACCESSIBLE KITCHEN
You do not need a special kitchen. You do need a consistent layout, clear counters, and a few tools. Give everything a home. Label jars and stove settings with bump dots, braille, or a talking labeler. Use a damp towel under the cutting board, a cut-resistant glove on the hand that holds the food, and the claw grip when you cut. Measure with tactile cups and spoons, a liquid level indicator, or a talking scale. Use a talking or instant-read thermometer for meat, poultry, egg dishes, and leftovers. Use a talking timer every time food is on the heat.

The 13 Kitchen Basics chapters cover these habits in more detail.

FOOD SAFETY, IN BRIEF
The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service sums this up as Clean, Separate, Cook, and Chill. Wash hands and tools after they touch raw meat, poultry, or eggs. Keep raw meat away from food that will not be cooked again. Cook to a safe internal temperature, measured in the thickest part:

- All poultry, including turkey bacon: 165 degrees F
- Ground beef and other ground meats: 160 degrees F
- Steaks, chops, and roasts of beef, pork, or lamb: 145 degrees F, then rest 3 minutes
- Fish: 145 degrees F
- Eggs and egg dishes: cook eggs until the yolk and white are firm; cook egg dishes to 160 degrees F
- Leftovers and casseroles: 165 degrees F

Refrigerate perishable leftovers within 2 hours, or within 1 hour if the room is hotter than 90 degrees F. Eat refrigerated leftovers within 3 to 4 days, and reheat them to 165 degrees F. The Kitchen Basics chapter "Food Safety and USDA Temperatures" is the full reference.
