ACCESSICOOK
Boneless Wings
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
Boneless wings are bite-size pieces of boneless chicken, coated, cooked until crisp, and tossed in a buttery hot sauce. They are not true wings. They are closer to a sauced chicken tender or nugget, which makes them easier to cut, easier to eat, and easier to check with a thermometer.

This version is baked, which is the safer teaching method. You still get a crunchy coating and a familiar buffalo-style sauce, without a pot of hot oil on the stove. A stovetop fry is described later as a variation, with extra safety notes, for when you want that style.

SERVINGS
4 as an appetizer, or 2 as a main dish

TOTAL TIME
About 50 minutes, including 15 minutes of prep and 16 to 20 minutes in the oven. Add 20 minutes if you chill the breaded chicken before baking, which helps the coating stay put.

DIFFICULTY
Intermediate. You will cut raw chicken, work with a two-bowl breading setup, and use a hot oven. A thermometer is required for doneness.


EQUIPMENT
- A cutting board used only for raw poultry, or a board you will wash with soap and hot water before it touches anything else
- A sharp chef's knife
- A cut-resistant glove for the hand that holds the chicken
- Two shallow bowls, pie plates, or a bowl plus a large zip-top bag
- A rimmed baking sheet
- A wire rack that fits in the baking sheet, if you have one (this lets hot air move under the chicken and makes the coating crisper)
- Parchment paper or foil, to line the pan
- Cooking spray or a little oil and a pastry brush
- A talking or instant-read meat thermometer
- A tactile or talking timer
- Measuring cups and spoons with tactile markings
- A liquid measuring cup and a liquid level indicator, for the milk if you use it, and for leftover sauce
- A small saucepan, for the sauce
- A large mixing bowl, for tossing the cooked chicken with sauce
- Tongs or a fork
- Long oven mitts
- A splatter screen, if you use the fried variation
- A fry or probe thermometer that can read oil temperature, if you use the fried variation


INGREDIENTS

Chicken and coating
- 1 pound boneless, skinless chicken breasts (or boneless, skinless chicken thighs)
- 2 large eggs
- 1 cup all-purpose flour
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided (use 1/2 teaspoon, which is half a teaspoon, in the flour, and keep the rest for the chicken)
- 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) garlic powder
- 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) paprika
- 1/4 teaspoon (a quarter teaspoon) to 1/2 teaspoon cayenne pepper, optional, depending on how much heat you want in the coating
- 1/4 teaspoon (a quarter teaspoon) black pepper
- Oil spray, or about 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Buffalo-style sauce
- 1/3 cup (a third of a cup) cayenne hot sauce, such as Frank's RedHot or a similar vinegar-based sauce
- 3 tablespoons unsalted butter
- 1 teaspoon honey or a pinch of sugar, optional, to soften the sharp edge of the sauce
- 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) Worcestershire sauce, optional

For serving, optional
- Ranch or blue cheese dressing, for dipping
- Celery sticks or carrot sticks

TACTILE TIPS
Chicken breasts feel smooth, slightly damp, and firm, with a tapered end. If a breast is very thick in the middle, you will slice it horizontally or pound it so the pieces cook evenly. Keep the package on a plate so juices do not run onto the counter.

Flour feels fine, dry, and silky, and it has almost no smell. Salt feels crystalline and heavier in the spoon. Garlic powder smells clearly of garlic. Paprika is a little coarser and smells sweet and slightly smoky. Cayenne smells sharper and can irritate your nose if you lean in close, so measure it carefully and wash your hands after you touch it.

Crack each egg into a small bowl of its own before adding it to the dipping bowl. That way, if a piece of shell falls in, you can fish it out with a spoon by feeling for the hard fragment.

Hot sauce is thin, sharp-smelling, and vinegary. Butter is firm when cold and is usually wrapped; unwrap it over the saucepan so it cannot slide away. If you measure hot sauce in a liquid cup, set a liquid level indicator on the rim so it beeps as the liquid rises.

To measure flour, spoon it into a dry measuring cup and level the top with a straight edge, feeling for the rim of the cup. Do not pack it down.


BEFORE YOU START
Set the oven rack in the middle position before you turn the oven on. Line the baking sheet with parchment or foil, and set the wire rack on it if you are using one. Lightly oil the rack or the lined pan so the coating does not stick.

Set out two zones on the counter: a raw-chicken zone for cutting and breading, and a clean landing zone for the finished baking sheet. Put a damp towel under the cutting board. Put on the cut-resistant glove. Decide where the hot pan will land when it comes out of the oven, and clear that space now.

Preheat the oven to 425 degrees F.


STEPS

1. Pat the chicken dry with paper towels. This helps the coating stick and reduces sputtering later.

2. On the raw-poultry board, use the claw grip: tuck your fingertips under, curl your fingers, and let the side of the knife rest against your knuckles. Cut the chicken into pieces about 1 1/2 to 2 inches long (one and a half to two inches). A useful tactile guide is the length of the first two joints of an adult index finger. Try to keep the pieces close to the same size so they finish together. If a breast is much thicker in the center than at the end, slice that thick part in half horizontally so you do not have a dense middle that stays undercooked.

3. Sprinkle the chicken lightly with the remaining salt.

4. In one shallow bowl, beat the eggs with a fork until the mixture feels even, with no large pockets of unmixed white.

5. In the second bowl or a large zip-top bag, combine the flour, 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) salt, garlic powder, paprika, black pepper, and cayenne if you are using it. Stir or shake until the seasonings are mixed through the flour. You should no longer feel clumps of spice sitting on top.

6. Working with a few pieces at a time, set the chicken in the egg and turn it until it is wet all over.

7. Lift a piece and let the extra egg drip back into the bowl.

8. Place it in the flour and press the flour on so the piece feels dry and sandy on all sides, with no slick, bare spots.

9. Shake off the loose flour. Set the coated pieces on the prepared rack or pan in a single layer, with a little space between them so the edges can crisp.

10. If you have time, refrigerate the breaded chicken for 20 minutes. The coating firms up and is less likely to slide off.

11. Spray the tops of the pieces lightly with oil, or brush on a thin film. You want the coating barely moistened, not soaked.

12. Wear long oven mitts. Place the pan on the middle rack. Set a talking timer for 10 minutes.

13. At 10 minutes, pull the rack partly out and turn each piece with tongs so the other side can brown. The coating should already feel drier and a little firm, not wet and pasty.

14. Return the pan and bake 6 to 10 minutes more.

15. Check the doneness cues in the next section. The chicken is safely done when a talking or instant-read thermometer inserted into the center of the thickest piece reads 165 degrees F. Check two or three pieces, especially any that felt larger when you cut them. Do not let the thermometer tip touch the pan, or the reading can run high. If any piece is under 165 degrees F, return the whole pan to the oven for 2 to 3 minutes and check again.

16. While the chicken bakes, add the hot sauce, butter, and optional honey and Worcestershire to a small saucepan.

17. Warm over low to medium-low heat, stirring, until the butter melts and the sauce feels smooth and even. You should hear only a faint, lazy bubble, not a hard boil. If the sauce spatters, the heat is too high. Take it off the heat and keep it warm.

18. Transfer the cooked chicken to a large bowl.

19. Pour on enough warm sauce to coat, then toss or fold with a spoon until the pieces feel evenly slick. Fold means turn the pieces over themselves gently.

20. Serve right away, with dressing and celery if you like. The coating is at its crispest in the first few minutes.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE

Non-visual cues: A thermometer in the center of the thickest piece must read 165 degrees F. The pieces feel firm when pressed with tongs, with a slight spring, not soft or squishy in the middle. The juices, if any, run clear, not thick and raw-feeling. The coating sounds dry and faintly crisp if you tap two pieces together, and it smells like cooked chicken and toasted flour, not raw meat. If you cut one thick piece open, use a clean knife, and the interior should feel set and fibrous, not pasty or gelatinous.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The coating should look dry and lightly golden, not wet and pasty, and not dark brown or burnt. The interior of a test piece should look white and opaque, not pink and raw, and the juices should look clear. A thermometer in the thickest piece should read 165 degrees F.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
These visual cues are for a sighted partner. They do not replace the thermometer or the non-visual cues in the steps. Call them out loud so both of you are working from the same information.

The breaded chicken should turn a light to medium golden brown, not pale and pasty and not dark brown or black in spots. When you cut a thick piece, the meat should be opaque and white or very light tan all the way through, with no raw pink or glassy look, and the juices should run clear. The sauce should coat the pieces evenly and look glossy, not watery in the bottom of the bowl.

SAFETY
Raw chicken can carry bacteria. Use a dedicated board, keep juices on a plate, and wash your hands, knife, tongs, and the board with soap and hot water after you finish breading, before you touch the sauce, the serving bowl, or any ready-to-eat food. Do not use the same tongs for raw and cooked chicken unless you wash them in between.

Use the claw grip and a cut-resistant glove when you cut. Confirm the knife's sharp edge faces down by feeling the spine (the dull top) first.

The baking sheet and the oven rack are hot enough to burn. Use long oven mitts, pull the rack partly out rather than reaching deep into the oven, and set the pan on a surface you cleared in advance. Turn off the oven when you are finished.

Hot sauce can splash and sting eyes and small cuts. Stir the sauce gently, and wash your hands after handling cayenne or hot sauce.

If you use the fried variation below, treat hot oil as a serious burn and fire hazard. See those notes before you start.


TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Oven-crisp coating: A wire rack is worth using if you have one. If you do not, turn the pieces once, as written, so the bottoms do not steam.

Mild or hot: For a milder sauce, use more butter or a little more honey. For more heat, add a pinch of cayenne to the sauce or use a hotter sauce. Taste a cooled drop of sauce on a clean spoon before you toss the chicken.

Other sauces: The same baked pieces work with barbecue sauce, a mild garlic-butter sauce, or a honey-mustard sauce. Warm thick sauces slightly so they coat evenly.

Thighs instead of breasts: Boneless thighs stay moist and are forgiving. They still need 165 degrees F in the center.

Air fryer: Cook in a single layer at 375 to 400 degrees F, turning once, until the thickest piece is 165 degrees F. Time varies by basket, often 12 to 16 minutes. Do not crowd the basket.

Stovetop fried variation (higher risk):
This is closer to many restaurant boneless wings, but it uses hot oil. Skip it until you are comfortable with the baked method and with thermometer use.

Refrigerate the breaded pieces for at least 20 minutes so the coating stays on. In a deep, heavy pot, add enough oil to come about 2 inches up the sides, leaving plenty of empty space above the oil. Heat the oil slowly over medium heat to 375 degrees F, measured with a fry or probe thermometer clipped or held so the tip is in the oil and not on the bottom of the pot. The oil should smell hot and faintly toasty, not sharp or acrid. If it smokes, it is too hot. Turn the heat off and let it cool.

Fry a few pieces at a time so the oil temperature does not drop hard and so the pot cannot bubble over. The oil will hiss and sizzle when the chicken goes in. Use long tongs, wear rolled-up sleeves, and keep a splatter screen handy. Cook about 5 to 7 minutes, turning once, until the coating feels firm and a thermometer in the center of a piece reads 165 degrees F. If the sizzle fades to almost nothing too quickly, the oil may have cooled; let it return to temperature between batches. Drain on a rack, then toss with sauce.

Never leave hot oil unattended. Keep a lid nearby. If the oil catches fire, turn off the heat and slide a lid over the pot. Do not throw water on oil, and do not try to carry a flaming pot. Keep water, ice, and wet chicken away from the oil, because water makes hot oil pop and spatter.


LEFTOVERS
Refrigerate leftover chicken within 2 hours, in a shallow container, for 3 to 4 days. The coating will soften in the refrigerator. Reheat in a 375 degree F oven or air fryer until the center reaches 165 degrees F. Microwaving works for safety but leaves the coating soft.
