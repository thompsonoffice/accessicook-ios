ACCESSICOOK
Bacon
Regular Pork Bacon and Turkey Bacon
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
This page covers two breakfast staples that look similar in the package and cook in similar pans, but they are not the same food. Regular bacon is cured pork belly. It is fatty, it renders a lot of grease, and it is thin enough that a thermometer is often hard to use. Turkey bacon is made from turkey. It is leaner, it can stick, and it is poultry, so it needs to reach 165 degrees F whenever you can get a reliable reading.

Both are usually sold uncooked and need to be cooked thoroughly before you eat them. Neither one should be judged by color alone. Cured pork bacon can stay pink even when it is fully cooked, and turkey bacon can brown before the interior is hot enough. You will go by texture, sound, smell, time, and, for turkey bacon, a thermometer.

The oven method is the easiest to control and the least spattery. The skillet method is faster for a few slices and teaches you the sound of fat rendering. Both methods are included for each kind of bacon.

SERVINGS
About 4 servings. Use 8 to 12 slices of pork bacon, or 8 to 12 slices of turkey bacon, depending on appetite and how crisp you like it. The two kinds should be cooked in separate pans or at separate times, not mixed, because their fat, timing, and safe temperatures differ.

TOTAL TIME
Skillet: about 15 to 20 minutes, including turning.
Oven, pork bacon: about 20 to 25 minutes at 400 degrees F, including a few minutes to preheat.
Oven, turkey bacon: about 20 to 25 minutes at 375 degrees F.

DIFFICULTY
Beginner to intermediate. The steps are simple. The hazards are hot grease, spatters, and, with turkey bacon, making sure poultry is fully cooked.


EQUIPMENT
- A large, heavy skillet, for the stovetop method (cast iron or a heavy nonstick both work)
- A rimmed baking sheet, for the oven method
- Foil or parchment, to line the baking sheet
- A wire rack that fits the baking sheet, optional, so fat can drip away
- Tongs
- A splatter screen
- Long oven mitts
- A tactile or talking timer
- A talking or instant-read meat thermometer, especially for turkey bacon
- A plate lined with paper towels or a clean brown paper bag, for draining
- A heat-safe jar or metal can, for cooled grease (never pour grease down the sink)
- Optional: a lid that fits the skillet, kept nearby in case of a grease flare-up
- Optional: a little oil or cooking spray, for turkey bacon, which is lean and can stick


INGREDIENTS
- 8 to 12 slices regular pork bacon (standard or thick-cut)
- or 8 to 12 slices turkey bacon
- Cooking spray or about 1 teaspoon oil, for turkey bacon only, if your pan or sheet tends to stick

Do not cook the two kinds in the same pan at the same time. If you want both on the same plate, cook them one after the other and keep the cooked bacon warm in a low oven, about 200 degrees F, on a rack or paper-towel-lined plate.

TACTILE TIPS
In the package, both products are thin, cool, and flexible, with slices that can be peeled apart. Pork bacon usually feels fattier and more slippery, with firmer streaks of fat along a slice. Thick-cut pork bacon is obviously thicker and takes longer. Turkey bacon often feels leaner, drier, and a little more uniform, and it may have a slightly tighter, rubbery bend when raw.

If the slices are stuck together, peel them apart slowly while they are still cold. If they tear, you can still cook the pieces; they will just be smaller.

Read the package with a reader app or a helper the first time you buy a new brand. Some turkey bacon is labeled with skillet and oven times. Those times are a starting point. You will still finish by texture and, when possible, temperature.

Keep raw bacon on a plate so its juices do not run onto bread, fruit, or anything that will not be cooked.


BEFORE YOU START
Clear a landing space for a hot pan or baking sheet. Set out tongs, a splatter screen, and the paper-towel-lined plate. If you are using the oven, place the rack in the center of the oven before you preheat, and line the baking sheet.

Do not leave bacon cooking unattended. Grease can pop, and thin slices can go from done to burnt in a short time.

If you are cooking pork bacon, plan now for the leftover grease. Let it cool in the pan, then pour it into a heat-safe jar, not down the drain.


STEPS FOR REGULAR PORK BACON

Skillet method (pork)

1. Lay the slices in a cold skillet in a single layer. The slices can touch slightly but should not be stacked. Starting in a cold pan lets the fat render more evenly and can reduce aggressive spatters. Render means the fat melts out of the meat slowly.

2. Turn the heat to medium.

3. After a minute or two you will hear a gentle sizzle, then a steadier frying sound as fat melts into the pan. Place a splatter screen over the skillet.

4. Cook, turning every couple of minutes with tongs, for about 8 to 12 minutes for regular slices, or 12 to 16 minutes for thick-cut. Time varies by stove and by how crisp you want the bacon. Use the doneness cues in the next section.

5. Transfer the slices to the paper-towel-lined plate to drain. They will crisp a little more as they cool.

6. Turn off the heat. Let the grease cool before you move the pan or pour it off.

Oven method (pork)

1. Heat the oven to 400 degrees F. Heat the oven, sometimes called preheat, means bring it to that temperature before the food goes in.

2. Line a rimmed baking sheet with foil or parchment. For easier cleanup and slightly drier bacon, set a wire rack in the pan and lay the slices on the rack. Otherwise, lay the slices directly on the lined pan in a single layer, not stacked.

3. Bake 15 to 20 minutes, until the slices feel firm and crisp, with the same texture and smell cues as the skillet method. You usually do not need to turn oven bacon, especially on a rack. If you are cooking directly on the pan and the bottoms feel greasy and soft at 12 minutes, you can turn the slices once with tongs, using long mitts and pulling the rack partly out.

4. Remove the pan with long mitts.

5. Transfer the bacon to paper towels.

6. Let the fat cool in the pan before you discard or save it.

STEPS FOR TURKEY BACON

Turkey bacon is poultry. USDA guidance is to cook all poultry to 165 degrees F. Slices are thin, so a single slice can be hard to probe. Use one or more of these methods: fold a slice loosely and insert the thermometer tip into the thickest part without touching the pan; stack two cooked slices briefly and check the center; or, if the product is thicker, check a slice from the side. If you cannot get a trustworthy reading, cook until the slices are hot all the way through, firm, and crisp, and extend the time rather than serving them soft in the middle.

Skillet method (turkey)

1. If your skillet is not very nonstick, add a thin film of oil or a mist of cooking spray.

2. Lay the turkey bacon in a single layer. You can start in a cold skillet, as many turkey-bacon packages recommend, or in a skillet already at medium heat. Either way, do not crowd the pan.

3. Cook over medium heat, turning often, for about 7 to 9 minutes, or until the slices are done as described in the next section. Turkey bacon has less fat, so it can go from pale and flexible to dry and brittle quickly. Stay with the pan and use a splatter screen. There will be less grease than with pork, but there can still be some spatter.

4. Drain on paper towels and serve.

Oven method (turkey)

1. Heat the oven to 375 degrees F. Some brands say not to preheat and to put the pan into a cold oven. If your package says that, follow it and start your timer when you turn the oven on, then still finish by texture and temperature. If there is no special instruction, heating the oven first is fine.

2. Line a baking sheet with foil and coat it lightly with cooking spray, or use a lightly oiled rack.

3. Lay the slices in a single layer.

4. Bake about 16 to 19 minutes, until the slices are crisp to your taste and a thermometer reading, if you can get one, is 165 degrees F. Turning is usually not necessary.

5. Remove the pan with long mitts and drain the bacon on paper towels.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE

Non-visual cues for pork bacon: The slices go from floppy and raw-feeling to firmer, then to crisp. When you lift a slice with tongs, it should hold its shape and feel rigid along the edges, not limp and heavy with unrendered fat. The sizzle changes. At first it is uneven and can sputter as water leaves the meat. As the bacon finishes, the sound becomes a drier, steadier fry, then quiets as less moisture remains. The kitchen should smell like cooked, smoky pork, not sour or burnt. A sharp, acrid smell means the pan is too hot or the bacon is scorching. Reduce the heat. A thermometer is often impractical on a thin slice, because there is not enough depth for the probe. If you use thick-cut bacon and can insert the tip sideways into a folded slice without touching the pan, cook until the bacon is crisp throughout. Color is not a reliable test. Cured bacon may remain pink after it is fully cooked. Cook pork bacon until it is thoroughly done and crisp to your taste. Do not stop at a soft, under-rendered, raw-feeling center.

Non-visual cues for turkey bacon: The slices should feel firm and heated through, with no cool, raw, or pasty center when you press or cut one. A thermometer should read 165 degrees F in the thickest area you can measure. The edges should feel drier and more rigid. You can cook it to a softer chew or to a crisp snap, but it should not feel undercooked and rubbery-cool in the middle. The smell should be cooked and savory, like turkey and smoke, not raw poultry.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: Pork bacon usually turns a deep golden to reddish brown and looks crisp and rendered, but curing can leave some pink even when it is fully cooked, so color alone is not a safety test. The fat should look clear and melted, not thick and raw-white in the middle of a slice. Turkey bacon should look evenly browned and cooked through, with no dull, raw, rubbery center. Because turkey bacon is poultry, confirm 165 degrees F whenever you can get a thermometer reading.

These visual cues are for a sighted partner. They do not replace the thermometer or the non-visual cues in the steps. Call them out loud so both of you are working from the same information.

SAFETY
Raw pork bacon and raw turkey bacon should be treated like other raw meat and poultry. Wash your hands, tongs, the plate that held the raw slices, and the counter with soap and hot water before you touch bread, fruit, cooked food, or your face.

Hot grease is the main injury risk. Use a splatter screen. Keep your face and forearms back when you turn slices. Wear short sleeves or rolled sleeves. Keep children and pets away from the stove.

If grease catches fire, turn off the heat and slide a lid over the pan to smother the flame. Do not throw water on a grease fire. Do not carry a flaming pan to the sink.

The oven pan and the rendered fat are both hot enough to burn. Use long mitts. Set the pan on a reserved landing space. Let fat cool before you pour it into a jar. Do not pour grease down the sink; it can solidify in the pipes.

Do not leave raw bacon out on the counter. Keep it refrigerated at 40 degrees F or below, and use opened packages within about 7 days, or freeze them. Cooked bacon should not sit out more than 2 hours (1 hour if the room is hotter than 90 degrees F).


TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Crispness: Bacon continues to firm as it cools for a minute or two on paper towels. If you want it very crisp, cook a little longer, but stay close at the end.

Thick-cut pork bacon: Give it more time, and use the oven if you want more even results with less turning.

Microwave: This works in a pinch for a few slices. Place slices in a single layer on a microwave-safe plate lined with paper towels, cover with another towel, and cook in short bursts, turning or rotating as your oven requires, until the texture cues above are met. Times vary widely. Watch for steam and for paper that can overheat. Turkey bacon still needs to be fully hot throughout.

Air fryer: A single layer at 350 to 375 degrees F often takes about 6 to 10 minutes for pork and about 8 to 10 minutes for turkey, turning once if your basket tends to cook unevenly. Check early. Finish turkey bacon at 165 degrees F when you can measure it.

Breakfast plate: These methods pair well with the omelette sample in this folder. Cook the bacon first, then the omelette, so the eggs get your full attention.

Saving pork bacon fat: Strained, cooled bacon fat can be stored in the refrigerator in a labeled jar and used later to fry eggs or potatoes. Treat it like other perishable fat and keep it refrigerated.


LEFTOVERS
Refrigerate cooked bacon within 2 hours, wrapped or in a container, for 3 to 4 days, or freeze it. Reheat until it is steaming hot and, for leftover turkey bacon, until it reaches 165 degrees F.
