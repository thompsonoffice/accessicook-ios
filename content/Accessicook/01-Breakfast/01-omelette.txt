ACCESSICOOK
Omelette
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
An omelette is beaten eggs cooked quickly in butter in a small skillet, then folded into a soft, savory oval. It is one of the fastest real meals you can make, and it is also a skill recipe: the pan is hot, the timing is short, and your hands and ears tell you more than a clock does.

Classic restaurant omelettes are often left slightly creamy in the center. This page aims for a fully set omelette that meets USDA guidance for egg dishes: 160 degrees F in the center, with no liquid raw egg remaining. The result is a little firmer than a traditional French omelette, and it is the safer standard for a teaching cookbook. Once you know the motions, you can make one in a few minutes on a busy morning.

SERVINGS
1 omelette (a hearty breakfast or a light meal). Double the eggs and use a 10-inch skillet only after you are comfortable with the basic method. It is easier to make two separate omelettes than one large one.

TOTAL TIME
About 10 to 15 minutes, including setup. The cooking itself takes about 2 to 4 minutes.

DIFFICULTY
Intermediate at first, then easy with practice. The technique is simple, but it happens quickly. Read the whole recipe once before you heat the pan.


EQUIPMENT
- An 8 inch nonstick skillet with sloped sides and a handle you can hold securely. A well-seasoned omelette pan also works. A sticky pan will fight you.
- A medium mixing bowl
- A fork or a small whisk
- A heat-safe silicone or rubber spatula (a fork can work in a nonstick pan if you are gentle)
- A talking or instant-read thermometer
- A tactile or talking timer, useful as a backup, though you will cook mostly by feel and sound
- Measuring spoons with tactile markings
- A plate, set next to the stove before you start
- Optional: a small bowl for cracked eggs, and a grater if you are adding cheese
- Long sleeves rolled above the elbow, so they cannot catch the handle or dip into the pan


INGREDIENTS
- 3 large eggs
- 1/8 teaspoon (an eighth of a teaspoon, a small pinch) salt, or to taste
- A few grindings of black pepper, or a pinch
- 1 tablespoon unsalted butter
- 1 tablespoon water or milk, optional, for a slightly more tender curd

Optional fillings, already prepared and at room temperature or gently warmed, about 1/4 cup (a quarter cup) total so the omelette still folds:
- Shredded cheese, such as cheddar or Swiss
- Cooked, crumbled bacon or diced ham that has already been heated
- Finely chopped tender herbs, such as chives or parsley
- A spoonful of cooked vegetables that are already soft and not wet

TACTILE TIPS
A raw egg in the shell is oval, smooth, and cool. Crack each egg on a flat counter or the rim of a small bowl, then drop it into that small bowl and feel for shell fragments with a spoon before you add it to the mixing bowl. A stray piece of shell feels hard and sharp against the spoon or your clean finger.

Beaten eggs feel uniformly slick and slightly thick, with no separate slippery whites floating among the yolks. Salt is crystalline. Butter is firm when cold; it will melt and then foam in the pan.

If you add cheese, pre-shredded cheese is easy to feel and measure. If you grate your own, a talking scale or a 1/4-cup measure both work. Keep fillings dry. Wet tomatoes or leftover vegetables will make the eggs watery and harder to set.

This recipe uses raw eggs. Use clean, uncracked eggs from the refrigerator, and cook them through as written.


BEFORE YOU START
This dish moves quickly. Set the plate by the stove. Beat the eggs, measure the butter, and have any fillings ready before the pan gets hot. If you are new to the motion of folding and sliding the omelette out, you can practice once with the empty, cold pan and a dry plate: tilt the pan over the plate and turn it as if you were unmolding the eggs. Then start cooking.

Use a burner that you can stand square to, and turn the handle of the skillet to the side, inward, so you cannot bump it.


STEPS

1. Crack the eggs into a bowl.

2. Add the salt, pepper, and the optional water or milk.

3. Beat with a fork or whisk just until the yolks and whites are combined and the mixture feels even. You do not need a frothy foam. Overbeating can make the omelette tough.

4. Set the 8 inch skillet over medium or just-under-medium heat.

5. Add the butter and swirl or tilt the pan so the butter coats the bottom and a little of the sides.

6. Wait until the butter melts, foams, and then settles, and you can hear a soft, lively sizzle. The butter should smell nutty but not browned or acrid. That is your cue that the pan is ready. If the butter smells burnt or the foam goes straight to a sharp, dark smell, take the pan off the heat, wipe it out with a paper towel once it is cool enough to handle, and start again with fresh butter at a slightly lower heat.

7. Pour the eggs into the pan. They should sizzle gently, not violently.

8. Right away, stir with the spatula or a fork, keeping the tool moving around the bottom of the pan and shaking the pan gently with your other hand. Soft curds will start to form. At first the mixture still sloshes like a liquid. Within a minute or so, it will feel thicker, like very soft, wet scrambled eggs that are starting to hold together.

9. When the eggs are mostly set but the top still feels slightly moist, stop stirring and spread the eggs into an even layer. The top should no longer flow like raw egg, though it may still feel a little damp.

10. If you are using fillings, sprinkle them over one half of the omelette now.

11. Give the pan another 15 to 30 seconds on the heat so the bottom firms enough to fold. Fold means lift one edge and lay it over the other half. If the bottom starts to smell browned or sharp, the heat is too high. Lift the pan off the burner for a few seconds.

12. Check the doneness cues in the next section. If the center is still under 160 degrees F or still feels liquid, cook a little longer over lower heat, or finish with a lid on the pan for 15 to 30 seconds so steam can set the top without browning the bottom. Then check again.

13. To fold, tilt the pan and use the spatula to lift one edge of the omelette and fold it over the other half, making a half-moon.

14. Let it sit for a few seconds so the fold holds.

15. Hold the plate next to the skillet, tilt the pan, and slide or invert the omelette onto the plate, seam side down if you can. Serve right away.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE

Non-visual cues: The omelette is done when the center is set, with no liquid egg remaining, and a thermometer inserted into the thickest part reads 160 degrees F. The surface should feel moist but not wet or slippery with raw egg. The omelette should smell gently cooked and buttery, not sulfurous or scorched.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The omelette should look pale yellow, set, and moist, with no shiny pools of raw egg on top and no large brown scorch patches on the bottom unless the heat was too high. When folded, it should hold a half-moon or oval shape. If you cut it, the center should look cooked and opaque, not liquid and translucent. A thermometer in the thickest part should read 160 degrees F.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
These visual cues are for a sighted partner. They do not replace the thermometer or the non-visual cues in the steps. Call them out loud so both of you are working from the same information.

SAFETY
Raw eggs can carry bacteria. Wash your hands, the bowl, and the fork after you beat the eggs, and cook the omelette to 160 degrees F. Do not taste raw beaten egg.

The skillet handle and the pan itself get hot. Keep the handle turned inward, but not over another burner. Use a dry mitt if the handle heats up. Oil and butter can spatter; stand back when the eggs go in, and do not flick water into the pan.

If you add meat fillings, they should already be cooked. Leftover meat or bacon belongs in the omelette only after it has been cooked and, if it was refrigerated, reheated or at least brought into the hot pan with the eggs until the whole omelette reaches 160 degrees F.

Clean the skillet after cooking. For a nonstick pan, wipe it out or wash it gently once it has cooled a little. Do not leave raw egg sitting in the pan.


TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Heat control is the whole skill. If the eggs set on the bottom in a few seconds and smell browned, the pan is too hot. If they sit there with no sizzle and no curds after a minute, the pan is too cool. Adjust and try again. Eggs are inexpensive, and a second attempt is part of learning.

Cheese omelette: Add the cheese just before you fold, so it softens but does not scorch on the pan.

Western or Denver-style: Fold in a small amount of already-cooked diced pepper, onion, and ham. Cook the vegetables first in a separate pan until they are soft, then add them as a filling.

Herb omelette: Stir chopped chives, parsley, or dill into the beaten eggs.

Larger appetite: Make a second omelette rather than crowding six eggs into the same 8 inch pan.

If the omelette sticks: The pan is not nonstick enough, or it was not buttered well, or the heat was too high. Do not scrape aggressively. Loosen what you can with a spatula, scramble the rest, and still cook it to 160 degrees F. It will still taste good, even if it is not a neat fold.


LEFTOVERS
Omelettes are best eaten immediately. If you do have leftovers, refrigerate them within 2 hours and eat them within 3 to 4 days. Reheat until the center reaches 165 degrees F.
