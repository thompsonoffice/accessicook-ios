Beef Tips over Mashed Potatoes and Gravy
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
This is a slow, savory braise: bite-size pieces of beef simmered in a brown gravy until they go from chewy to tender, served over buttery mashed potatoes. It is a good teaching recipe for two skills at once, browning meat and then cooking it gently in liquid, plus a straightforward pot of mashed potatoes.

The beef is done when a fork or the tip of a knife slides into a piece with little resistance, not merely when it has reached the USDA minimum temperature. Stew-size pieces of beef are safely cooked at 145 degrees F with a 3-minute rest, but they will still be tough at that point. You will keep simmering until the meat is tender, which takes well over an hour.

SERVINGS
4 generous main-dish servings (6 smaller ones)

TOTAL TIME
About 2 hours 30 minutes to 3 hours, including 30 minutes of prep. Most of that time is hands-off simmering. The mashed potatoes take about 30 minutes and can be made near the end of the braise.

DIFFICULTY
Intermediate. There is knife work, a hot Dutch oven, and two pots going near the end. The method itself is steady and forgiving.


EQUIPMENT
- A large cutting board, with a damp towel underneath so it cannot slide
- A sharp chef's knife
- A cut-resistant glove
- A heavy Dutch oven or a wide, heavy pot with a lid
- A second large pot, for the potatoes
- A colander, for draining potatoes
- A potato masher or ricer
- A wooden spoon or heat-safe spatula
- Tongs
- A talking or instant-read meat thermometer
- A tactile or talking timer
- Measuring cups and spoons with tactile markings
- A liquid measuring cup and a liquid level indicator
- Long oven mitts or sturdy pot holders
- A ladle or large spoon, for serving
- Optional: a talking kitchen scale, for the beef and potatoes
- Optional: a vegetable peeler


INGREDIENTS

For the beef tips and gravy
- 2 to 2 1/2 pounds (two to two and a half pounds) beef chuck roast, cut into 1 to 1 1/2 inch (one to one and a half inch) pieces, or the same weight of already-cut stew beef
- 1 1/2 teaspoons (one and a half teaspoons) kosher salt, or 1 teaspoon table salt, plus more later if needed
- 1 teaspoon black pepper
- 1 teaspoon dried thyme, or 2 teaspoons fresh thyme leaves
- 2 tablespoons vegetable oil, or another neutral oil, divided
- 2 tablespoons unsalted butter
- 1 medium yellow onion, diced
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 1/4 cup (a quarter cup) all-purpose flour
- 3 cups low-sodium beef broth
- 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
- 1 tablespoon soy sauce, optional but helpful for a deeper, savory gravy
- 2 tablespoons chopped fresh parsley, optional, for serving

For the mashed potatoes
- 2 pounds Yukon Gold or russet potatoes
- 1 tablespoon salt, for the cooking water, plus more to taste
- 4 tablespoons unsalted butter
- 1/2 cup (half a cup) whole milk or half-and-half, warmed
- Black pepper, to taste

TACTILE TIPS
Chuck roast feels well marbled: you will notice softer fat seams running through firmer muscle. Stew beef is already in cubes, which saves cutting time, though the pieces can be uneven. Trim off only large, hard chunks of fat or silver skin; some fat is useful for flavor.

An onion is round and papery. After you cut off the root and stem ends and peel away the dry skin, the flesh is slick and layered. Garlic cloves are smaller, with a tighter paper skin that slips off more easily if you crush the clove lightly under the flat of the knife (press down on the blade spine, never on the sharp edge).

Flour is fine and dry. Beef broth is thin and smells meaty. Worcestershire sauce is thin, dark, and sharp, with a tamarind and anchovy smell. Soy sauce is salty and rounded. Keep both bottles labeled.

Yukon Gold potatoes are medium, smooth, and slightly thin-skinned. Russets are larger, with a rougher, drier skin. Either works. After peeling, the surface is damp and slightly starchy. Warm the milk in a small pan or in the microwave until it feels hot to a clean finger held just above the surface, or until a thermometer reads about 120 to 140 degrees F. Cold milk makes the mash gluey and cools it too fast.

Measure broth with a liquid cup and a liquid level indicator so you can hear when you near 3 cups.


BEFORE YOU START
Pat the beef dry and season it before you heat the pot. Dice the onion and mince the garlic, and set them aside in small bowls. Measure the flour, broth, Worcestershire, and soy sauce so they are ready when the onions are cooked. Clear a landing space for the hot pot. If your stove has more than one burner, plan to keep the potatoes on a back or side burner so you are not reaching over the Dutch oven.


STEPS

1. If you are starting with a roast, place it on the board and cut it into roughly even cubes, about 1 to 1 1/2 inches (one to one and a half inches). Use the claw grip and a cut-resistant glove. A cube about the size of a large walnut or the first joint of your thumb is a good guide. If the beef is already cubed, skip the cutting.

2. Toss the beef in a bowl with the salt, pepper, and thyme until the pieces feel evenly dusted.

3. Heat 1 tablespoon of the oil in the Dutch oven over medium-high heat until the oil smells hot and a single piece of beef sizzles firmly when it touches the surface.

4. Add only enough beef to make a single layer with space between the pieces. If you crowd the pot, the meat will steam and will not brown.

5. Sear without moving the pieces for 2 to 3 minutes, until they release easily and smell deeply browned, not just steamed. Sear means cook the surface in a hot pan until it browns.

6. Turn and brown a second side the same way.

7. Transfer that batch to a plate and repeat with the remaining oil and beef. The bottom of the pot should have browned bits stuck to it. Those will flavor the gravy.

8. Reduce the heat to medium.

9. Add the butter and the diced onion to the pot. Dice means cut into small, even cubes.

10. Stir, scraping the bottom, and cook 3 to 5 minutes, until the onion feels softer, smells sweet, and no longer tastes raw and sharp if you test a cooled piece.

11. Add the garlic and cook about 30 seconds, until it is fragrant. Do not let the garlic stay on the hot surface long enough to smell bitter or sharp in a burnt way.

12. Sprinkle the flour over the onions.

13. Stir for about 1 minute, until the flour coats the onions and leaves a thin film on the bottom of the pot and the raw flour smell fades.

14. Slowly pour in the broth while you stir or whisk, scraping the bottom so the browned bits dissolve and no dry lumps of flour remain. Whisk means beat with a fork or a whisk. You can feel lumps with the back of a spoon. Press and stir until the liquid is smooth.

15. Stir in the Worcestershire and soy sauce.

16. Return the beef and any juices from the plate to the pot.

17. Bring the liquid up until you hear a steady boil, then reduce the heat so the pot stays at a gentle simmer. Simmer means small, regular bubbles, not a hard, rattling boil.

18. Cover and cook for 1 hour, stirring every 20 minutes or so and making sure nothing is sticking. A talking timer helps here.

19. After 1 hour, uncover the pot and keep simmering 45 minutes to 1 hour more, stirring now and then, until the beef is very tender and the gravy has thickened. Use the doneness cues in the next section.

20. Taste a cooled spoonful of gravy and add a pinch of salt only if it needs it. Low-sodium broth and soy sauce already contribute salt.

21. While the beef finishes, peel the potatoes if you like a smoother mash. A peeler is safer than a knife. Work away from your body and rotate the potato.

22. Cut them into roughly even chunks, about 1 1/2 to 2 inches (one and a half to two inches), so they cook at the same rate.

23. Put them in the second pot, add cold water to cover by about 1 inch, and add the 1 tablespoon of salt. Feel with a clean finger or a spoon for the water line before you turn on the heat, or use a liquid level indicator at the pot rim.

24. Bring to a boil over high heat, then reduce to a moderate boil so the pot does not splash.

25. Cook 15 to 20 minutes, until a piece is done. Use the potato cues in the next section.

26. Drain the potatoes well in a colander, then return them to the hot pot for a minute so leftover steam can escape.

27. Add the butter and the warmed milk.

28. Mash until the mixture feels smooth enough for your taste, with no hard lumps. Do not beat it long after it is smooth, or it can turn gluey.

29. Taste a cooled bite and add salt and pepper as needed. Cover to keep warm.

30. Spoon a bed of mashed potatoes into a bowl or plate and ladle the beef and gravy over the top. Sprinkle with parsley if you are using it. Serve hot.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE

Non-visual cues for the beef and gravy: A fork or the tip of a knife should slide into a piece of beef with little resistance. The meat should give and start to shred when you press it, not bounce back like a rubber eraser. A thermometer in the center of a piece will be well above 145 degrees F by this point. That minimum is for safety on whole-muscle beef. Tenderness is the real finishing cue for this dish. The gravy should feel silky and coat the back of a spoon. When you draw a finger (carefully, the spoon will be hot) or the edge of another spoon through that coating, it should leave a brief, clear trail. If the gravy is still thin and watery at the end, simmer a few more minutes uncovered. If it is too thick and pasty, stir in a splash of broth or water. The kitchen should smell browned, meaty, and savory, not sour or raw.

Non-visual cues for the potatoes: A fork or skewer should slide into the center of a chunk with no grit or hard core. The chunk should mash easily when pressed against the side of the pot. A thermometer in the center of a large chunk will often read around 205 to 210 degrees F when the potato is fully soft. The pieces may show slight cracks at the edges and will smell plainly of cooked potato, not raw and earthy.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The beef should look browned on the outside and dull and cooked inside, and it should come apart easily. The gravy should look brown and slightly thick, not watery and not pasty. The mashed potatoes should look fluffy and even, not gluey and shiny, and not dry with hard lumps.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
These visual cues are for a sighted partner. They do not replace the thermometer or the non-visual cues in the steps. Call them out loud so both of you are working from the same information.

The beef should be browned on the outside, not gray and steamed. In the finished gravy, the meat should look dull and cooked, not red or raw in the center. The gravy should be a medium-brown and thick enough to coat a spoon, not the color of weak broth. The mashed potatoes should look fluffy and even, with no hard, translucent lumps of undercooked potato.

SAFETY
Use the claw grip and a cut-resistant glove when you cut raw beef and when you cut potatoes. Keep raw beef and its juices away from the potatoes, butter, milk, and serving dishes until the beef is fully cooked. Wash the board, knife, and your hands with soap and hot water after cutting the meat.

The Dutch oven, its lid, and the potato pot will be very hot, and the gravy can bubble and spit as it thickens. Keep handles turned inward, use long mitts or sturdy pot holders, and lift the lid away from your face so steam does not roll toward you.

Never fill the potato pot so full that it boils over. If a boil sounds suddenly louder and you hear water hitting the burner, reduce the heat.

Refrigerate leftovers promptly, as described below. A big pot of gravy cools slowly, so divide it into shallow containers.


TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Better browning: Dry beef and uncrowded batches matter more than high heat. If the pot sounds like it is steaming instead of sizzling, wait, or brown fewer pieces at a time.

No soy sauce: You can leave it out. The gravy will still work. A teaspoon of tomato paste cooked with the flour is another way to deepen the flavor.

Mushrooms or carrots: Add sliced mushrooms after the onion softens, and cook until they give off liquid and start to shrink, or add thick carrot coins with the broth. They should be tender by the time the beef is tender.

Make-ahead: The beef and gravy often taste even better the next day. Reheat gently on the stove until the gravy bubbles and the beef is hot in the center, at least 165 degrees F if you are reheating leftovers. Add a splash of broth if the gravy has tightened in the refrigerator. The potatoes are best freshly mashed, but they can be reheated with a little extra milk.

Other starches: The same beef and gravy are good over egg noodles or rice if you want to skip the potatoes.


LEFTOVERS
Refrigerate within 2 hours, in shallow containers, for 3 to 4 days, or freeze the beef and gravy for up to 3 to 4 months. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F. Bring gravy back to a bubble on the stove.
