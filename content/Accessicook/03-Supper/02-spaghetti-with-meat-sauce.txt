ACCESSICOOK
Spaghetti with Meat Sauce
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
Spaghetti with meat sauce is a weeknight staple: a pot of ground beef simmered with onion, garlic, tomato paste, and crushed tomatoes, tossed with long pasta and a little of the starchy cooking water. It is a good teaching recipe for browning ground meat, judging a simmer by sound, and cooking pasta to a firm, pleasant bite without looking at it.

This is an American-style meat sauce, not a long Sunday Bolognese. It is ready in under an hour, and the sauce can be made ahead.

SERVINGS
4 to 6

TOTAL TIME
About 45 to 55 minutes, including 15 minutes of prep and a 15- to 20-minute simmer.

DIFFICULTY
Beginner to intermediate. The knife work is simple, and the timing is relaxed, but you will manage two pots and a pot of boiling water.


EQUIPMENT
- A cutting board, with a damp towel underneath
- A sharp chef's knife
- A cut-resistant glove
- A wide, heavy pot or a deep skillet with tall sides, for the sauce
- A large pot, for the pasta
- A colander
- A wooden spoon or heat-safe spatula
- A measuring cup for scooping out pasta water
- A liquid measuring cup and a liquid level indicator
- Measuring spoons with tactile markings
- A talking or instant-read meat thermometer
- A tactile or talking timer
- Long oven mitts or sturdy pot holders
- Tongs or a pasta fork, for turning and serving the spaghetti
- A ladle
- Optional: a talking kitchen scale, and a box grater or a bowl of pre-grated Parmesan


INGREDIENTS
- 1 tablespoon olive oil
- 1 medium yellow onion, finely diced
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 1 pound ground beef (85 or 90 percent lean is a good middle ground)
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided, plus more for the pasta water
- 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) black pepper
- 2 tablespoons tomato paste
- 1 teaspoon dried oregano
- 1 teaspoon dried basil, or 2 teaspoons Italian seasoning in place of the oregano and basil
- 1/4 teaspoon (a quarter teaspoon) red pepper flakes, optional
- 1 can (28 ounces) crushed tomatoes
- 1/2 cup (half a cup) water or low-sodium beef broth, if the sauce gets tighter than you like
- 1 pound dried spaghetti
- 1/2 cup (half a cup) grated Parmesan cheese, plus more for serving
- A little chopped fresh basil or parsley, optional

TACTILE TIPS
Ground beef is cool, damp, and packed. Keep it on a plate until it goes in the pot. Onion and garlic are described in the beef-tips recipe in this folder: peel the papery skins, use the claw grip, and keep the pieces fairly small so they soften quickly. If chopping onion is uncomfortable at first, a food chopper or already-diced onion from the store is fine.

Tomato paste is thick, sticky, and concentrated, usually from a small can or tube. Crushed tomatoes are wet and slightly chunky in the can; use a liquid level indicator only if you transfer them to a cup, or simply empty the whole can into the pot. Dried oregano and basil are leafy and fragrant; Italian seasoning is a blend with a similar smell. Parmesan is dry, granular, and salty. Pre-grated cheese is the easiest to measure by the handful or with a 1/2-cup measure.

Dried spaghetti is a tight, long bundle. Before the water is hot, you can feel how much of the box you are using. For 1 pound, use the full standard box.

Salt for pasta water should be generous. A common teaching cue is that the water should taste plainly salty, like seawater, once the salt dissolves. Add the salt after the water is hot, so it dissolves quickly, and keep your face away from the steam.


BEFORE YOU START
Dice the onion and mince the garlic first, then wash your hands and set those bowls aside. Measure the tomato paste, herbs, and cheese. Open the can of tomatoes and set it near the stove. Fill the pasta pot with water before you start the sauce, but do not turn it on until the sauce is simmering, unless you are comfortable managing both at once.

Clear space for draining pasta. Set the colander in the sink before the pot of water boils. Make sure you can carry or slide the heavy pot safely, or use a smaller pot and cook the spaghetti in two batches if a full pot is hard to lift.


STEPS

1. Heat the olive oil in the wide pot over medium heat until it smells hot and flows easily when you tilt the pot.

2. Add the onion and a pinch of the salt.

3. Cook, stirring often, 4 to 6 minutes, until the onion feels soft, smells sweet, and no longer tastes sharp and raw if you try a cooled piece.

4. Add the garlic and cook about 30 seconds, until it is fragrant. If the garlic starts to smell bitter or sharp in a burnt way, reduce the heat.

5. Add the ground beef, the remaining salt, and the pepper.

6. Break the meat into small pieces with the spoon as it cooks. At first the beef is soft and pasty. As it cooks, it will firm up, smell savory, and stop feeling raw and slick. Keep stirring so the pieces brown rather than steam in a solid cake.

7. Cook until a thermometer in several pieces reads 160 degrees F, and no soft, raw-feeling meat remains. This usually takes 6 to 8 minutes.

8. If a lot of fat has collected in the pot and you prefer a less greasy sauce, push the meat to one side and spoon off some of the fat, or carefully tilt the pot and spoon from the edge. Leave a little fat for flavor.

9. Stir in the tomato paste, oregano, basil, and red pepper flakes if you are using them.

10. Cook 1 to 2 minutes, stirring, until the paste darkens slightly in smell, from bright and raw-tomato to deeper and caramelized. This step takes the canned edge off the sauce.

11. Pour in the crushed tomatoes and stir, scraping the bottom of the pot.

12. Bring the sauce up until you hear a gentle bubble, then reduce the heat to maintain a steady, quiet simmer, not a splattering boil. Simmer means small, gentle bubbles.

13. Set a talking timer for 15 minutes. Stir now and then so the sauce does not stick. Use the sauce cues in the next section.

14. You can keep the sauce on very low heat while the pasta cooks. If it starts to spit, turn the heat down and partly cover the pot, leaving a gap so steam can escape.

15. While the sauce simmers, bring the large pot of water to a rolling boil. You will hear a full, rolling bubble, and steam will rise strongly from the surface.

16. Add the salt, then add the spaghetti.

17. Fan the noodles with tongs or a spoon as they soften so they slide under the water and do not set in a stiff clump. Stir in the first 30 seconds, then once or twice more.

18. Cook according to the package time, then start testing about 1 minute earlier. To test, lift a strand, blow on it or hold it until it is cool enough, and bite it. Al dente pasta is fully flexible, cooked through, and still slightly firm in the center, with no hard, chalky, raw core. Al dente means "to the tooth," or tender with a slight firmness in the center. If you cut a cooled strand, you should feel even resistance, not a brittle middle.

19. Before you drain, dip a heat-safe mug or measuring cup into the pot and save about 1 cup of the starchy pasta water. The water will be hot. Use a mitt and set the cup back from the edge of the counter.

20. Drain the pasta in the colander.

21. Add the drained spaghetti to the sauce, or return the pasta to its pot and ladle the sauce over it.

22. Add about 1/2 cup (half a cup) of the reserved pasta water and the Parmesan. Reserved pasta water is the starchy cooking water you saved, and it helps the sauce cling to the noodles.

23. Toss with tongs until the noodles feel evenly coated and the sauce clings rather than sitting in a puddle at the bottom. If it seems dry or sticky, add more pasta water a little at a time.

24. Serve hot, with more Parmesan and herbs if you like.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE

Non-visual cues: The ground beef is safely done when a thermometer in several pieces reads 160 degrees F, and no soft, raw-feeling meat remains. After 15 to 20 minutes, the sauce should feel thicker. A spoon drawn through it should leave a brief trail, and it should mound a little on the spoon rather than running off like juice. The raw, tinny smell of canned tomato should have rounded into something sweeter and cooked. Taste a cooled spoonful and add salt only if it needs it. Parmesan at the end will add more salt. If the sauce gets too tight and pasty, stir in a splash of water or broth. If it is still thin at 20 minutes, simmer a few minutes more, uncovered. The pasta should feel fully flexible, cooked through, and still slightly firm in the center, with no hard, chalky, raw core.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The beef should look browned and broken into small pieces, with no raw pink remaining. The sauce should look thick enough to coat a spoon, not watery and not pasty. The pasta should look evenly coated, not dry and not sitting in a puddle of sauce.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
These visual cues are for a sighted partner. They do not replace the thermometer or the non-visual cues in the steps. Call them out loud so both of you are working from the same information.

The ground beef should be brown all the way through, with no pink, raw-looking spots. The sauce should look thick and slightly glossy, not thin and watery, and it should cling to the pasta. Cooked spaghetti should look flexible and slightly pale in the very center when you bite or cut a strand, not chalky white and stiff, and not falling apart. A thermometer in the browned beef should read 160 degrees F before you simmer it in the sauce; once mixed, leftovers must be reheated to 165 degrees F.

SAFETY
Ground beef must reach 160 degrees F. Use a thermometer in several spots, because a pan of ground meat does not always cook evenly. Wash your hands, the board, the knife, the package plate, and the spoon if it touched raw beef, with soap and hot water before you handle the cheese, the colander, or serving dishes.

Boiling pasta water is a burn hazard. Fill the pot no more than halfway to two-thirds full so it cannot boil over. Add the pasta carefully so the water does not splash. Keep handles turned inward. When you drain, stand back from the steam, and do not try to pour from a pot that is too heavy. If lifting a full pot is hard, use a mug or a small colander scoop to remove some water first, or cook only half a pound at a time.

Tomato sauce can bubble and spit as it thickens. Stir with a long spoon, and use a splatter screen or a partly set lid if needed.

Raw pasta water and sauce are both hot enough to burn. Keep the reserved-water cup where it cannot be knocked over.


TIPS AND VARIATIONS
Make-ahead sauce: The meat sauce can be refrigerated for 3 to 4 days or frozen for up to 3 to 4 months. Reheat it to a bubble, then toss with freshly cooked pasta and a splash of pasta water. Leftover combined spaghetti also keeps, though the noodles will be softer.

No red pepper: Leave out the flakes for a milder sauce.

Other meats: Ground turkey or a mix of beef and Italian sausage also works. Ground turkey is poultry and should reach 165 degrees F. Sausage should be cooked through, with no raw, pasty center; if it is a ground-meat sausage, use 160 degrees F for pork or beef sausage, or 165 for poultry sausage.

Extra vegetables: Finely chopped carrot or celery can go in with the onion. Let them soften before you add the beef. A handful of baby spinach can wilt in the sauce at the end.

Other pasta shapes: Rigatoni, penne, or linguine all carry this sauce well. Use the same doneness test.

If the pasta sticks: It needed more water, more stirring at the start, or a little more oil in a very starchy pot. Do not rinse the pasta for this recipe; the surface starch helps the sauce cling, and reserved pasta water is part of the method.


LEFTOVERS
Refrigerate within 2 hours, in shallow containers, for 3 to 4 days. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F. A splash of water in the pan or microwave keeps the noodles from drying out. Reheat sauce on the stove until it bubbles.
