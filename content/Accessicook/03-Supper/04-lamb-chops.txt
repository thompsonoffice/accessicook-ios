Lamb Chops
============================================================

INTRODUCTION
This recipe was on the original request list for the book.

Lamb chops cook quickly in a hot skillet and taste strongly of the meat itself, so a simple seasoning of salt, pepper, garlic, and herbs is enough. The USDA minimum for lamb chops is 145 degrees F, then a 3-minute rest. Rest means let cooked meat sit so the juices settle back into the meat. At that temperature the center may still look slightly pink, which is expected and safe when the thermometer confirms the number.

SERVINGS
2 to 4, depending on the size of the chops (about 4 loin chops or 6 to 8 smaller rib chops)

TOTAL TIME
About 25 minutes, plus 20 minutes to take the chill off the meat

DIFFICULTY
Intermediate

EQUIPMENT
- Measuring cups and spoons with tactile markings
- A liquid measuring cup and a liquid level indicator when you are measuring liquids
- A tactile or talking timer
- A talking or instant-read food thermometer
- A stable cutting board with a damp towel underneath
- A sharp chef's knife
- A cut-resistant glove for the hand that holds the food
- A large skillet or saute pan
- A heat-safe spatula or wooden spoon
- A splatter screen
- Tongs
- A plate for resting the chops
- Foil, to tent the meat while it rests

INGREDIENTS
- 4 lamb loin chops, about 1 inch thick, or 6 to 8 rib chops of similar thickness, about 1 1/2 pounds (one and a half pounds) total
- 1 teaspoon kosher salt, or 3/4 teaspoon (three-quarters of a teaspoon) table salt
- 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) black pepper
- 1 teaspoon dried rosemary or thyme, or 2 teaspoons chopped fresh
- 2 garlic cloves, minced, or 1/2 teaspoon (half a teaspoon) garlic powder
- 1 tablespoon olive oil
- 1 tablespoon unsalted butter, optional, for finishing
- Lemon wedges, optional, for serving

TACTILE TIPS
Loin chops feel like small T-bone steaks, with a bone in the middle and meat on both sides. Rib chops have a long bone handle and a round eye of meat. They should feel cold and slightly damp, and the fat along the edge should feel firm. Let them sit out 15 to 20 minutes so they are not ice-cold in the center, but do not leave raw meat out longer than needed.

BEFORE YOU START
Read the whole recipe once before you turn on the heat. Set out the equipment and ingredients. Clear a landing space for hot pans. If you will be cutting, put a damp towel under the cutting board. If you are cooking with a helper, decide who will handle knives, the stove, and the thermometer before you start.

STEPS
1. Pat the chops dry.

2. Mix the salt, pepper, rosemary or thyme, and garlic, and rub it over both sides of the meat.

3. Heat the olive oil in a large skillet over medium-high heat until the oil smells hot and a corner of a chop sizzles firmly when it touches the pan.

4. Lay the chops in the pan in a single layer, leaving space between them.

5. Cook, without moving them, for 3 to 4 minutes, until they release easily from the pan and smell browned, not steamed.

6. Turn the chops and cook 3 to 5 minutes more, depending on thickness.

7. Start checking temperature at 3 minutes on the second side.

8. Insert a thermometer into the thickest part of the meat, from the side if you can, without touching bone.

9. When the thermometer reads 145 degrees F, transfer the chops to a plate.

10. Add the optional butter to the hot pan and spoon the melted butter over the chops, or skip this step.

11. Tent loosely with foil and rest at least 3 minutes, then serve with lemon if you like.

HOW TO TELL WHEN IT IS DONE
Non-visual cues: A firm sizzle means the pan is hot enough; a steaming sound means it is crowded or too cool. Done chops feel springy-firm, not soft and raw, and not hard like overcooked meat. The fat should smell browned and savory, not scorched. A thermometer in the thickest meat, off the bone, should read 145 degrees F, then the chops should rest at least 3 minutes.

Visual cues for a sighted helper: The outside should be browned, with some darker, caramelized spots in the fat. At 145 degrees F the center may still look lightly pink, which is normal for lamb at the USDA minimum. There should be no cool, raw, dark-red look in the middle, and the juices should run only faintly pink or clear, not thick and bloody.

FOR THE SIGHTED HELPER
Say what you see out loud, especially color and browning. Share any thermometer reading with your partner. Do not skip the temperature checks in the steps. Visual cues help, but they do not replace a thermometer for meat, poultry, egg dishes, or leftovers. The outside should be browned, with some darker, caramelized spots in the fat. SAFETY
Use the claw grip only if you are trimming fat; many chops need no cutting before they go in the pan. Wash your hands and tongs after they touch raw lamb. The skillet and spattering fat are burn hazards; use a splatter screen and turn handles inward. Do not press hard on the chops or you will squeeze out juices and encourage flare-ups. For lamb chops and roasts, the USDA minimum is 145 degrees F, then a 3-minute rest. The center may still look slightly pink at this temperature.

TIPS AND VARIATIONS
If the chops are much thicker than 1 inch, finish them in a 400 degree F oven after searing both sides, still to 145 degrees F. For a mint finish, stir a little chopped mint into the butter before you spoon it on. Shoulder chops are often cheaper and a bit chewier; they can take another minute or two per side.

LEFTOVERS
Leftover lamb is good sliced cold in a salad or warmed gently. Reheat to 165 degrees F if you are serving it hot. Refrigerate perishable leftovers within 2 hours of cooking, or within 1 hour if the room is hotter than 90 degrees F. Use shallow containers so the food cools faster. Eat refrigerated leftovers within 3 to 4 days. Reheat leftovers to 165 degrees F. Bring leftover soups, sauces, and gravies back to a boil.
